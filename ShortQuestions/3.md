**Compare Lock-based synchronization and CAS (Compare-And-Swap). When would you choose one over the other?**

Lock-based synchronization relies on explicit locks or monitors to ensure that only one thread can access a critical section at a time. When a thread acquires a lock, other threads attempting to enter the same critical section are blocked until the lock is released. This guarantees mutual exclusion and makes reasoning about shared state easier. However, locks come with overhead and potential pitfalls: acquiring and releasing a lock involves system calls or CPU-level operations, which can degrade performance under high contention. Locks also introduce the risk of deadlocks if multiple locks are acquired in inconsistent orders, and can cause thread starvation where some threads are perpetually delayed.

CAS (Compare-And-Swap), on the other hand, is a lock-free atomic operation that allows a thread to update a variable only if it has not been changed by another thread since it was last read. CAS repeatedly attempts to make the change until it succeeds, which eliminates the need for traditional locks. Because CAS is non-blocking, it can provide better throughput under low to moderate contention and avoids deadlocks entirely. However, CAS can suffer from livelock, where multiple threads continuously interfere with each other, repeatedly failing to update the variable. CAS is also generally limited to updating a single memory location atomically, making it less convenient for complex operations on multiple shared variables.

Choosing between the two depends on the workload and complexity of the critical section. Lock-based synchronization is appropriate when the critical section is large, involves multiple shared variables, or when simplicity and correctness are more important than peak performance. CAS is better suited for fine-grained, high-performance scenarios, such as updating counters, implementing non-blocking queues, or other small, frequently-updated shared structures where the cost of locking would outweigh the risk of repeated CAS retries. Often, hybrid approaches are used, combining CAS for hot paths and locks for less frequent, complex operations to balance performance and maintainability.
