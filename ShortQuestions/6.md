**What is exponential backoff with jitter? Why is jitter important when implementing retry logic?**

Exponential backoff with jitter is a retry strategy used in distributed systems to handle transient failures (like timeouts, rate limits, or temporary service outages). The idea is simple: instead of retrying immediately after a failure, the client waits for a delay that grows exponentially with each retry attempt. For example, the wait time might be 100ms, then 200ms, then 400ms, then 800ms, and so on. This “exponential” growth reduces pressure on a struggling service and gives it time to recover. The “jitter” part means adding randomness to the delay rather than waiting for a fixed, deterministic time.

Without jitter, exponential backoff alone can still cause serious problems. Imagine thousands of clients all making the same request at the same time. If the service fails and all clients use the same backoff formula, they will retry at exactly the same intervals—100ms, 200ms, 400ms, etc. This creates synchronized retry waves, often called the “thundering herd” problem. Instead of reducing load, retries happen in spikes, which can repeatedly overwhelm the service and prevent it from recovering.

Jitter solves this by randomizing the wait time. Instead of waiting exactly 400ms, a client might wait anywhere between 0 and 400ms (full jitter), or 300–500ms (equal jitter), depending on the strategy. This spreads retry attempts over time, smoothing out traffic and significantly reducing the chance of coordinated spikes. As a result, the system becomes more stable, recovery is faster, and overall success rates improve under high contention.

In practice, jitter is especially important in cloud environments, microservices architectures, and systems using APIs with rate limits. When many clients or instances are deployed with identical logic, deterministic retries amplify failures. Randomization breaks that synchronization. So while exponential backoff reduces retry frequency over time, jitter ensures those retries are distributed, making the combination much more robust than exponential backoff alone.
